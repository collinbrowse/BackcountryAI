Source: WikiCiv (rebuilding technology) | https://wikiciv.org/wiki/AC_off-grid_photovoltaic_powered_open-source_ball_mill

Ball milling is used for comminution by rotating a drum to grind materials using balls with specific diameters. Ball milling advantages include the potential for high capacity, predicted fineness in a specific amount of time, reliability, safety, and simplicity, but has disadvantages of high weight, energy consumption and costs, which limit accessibility. To overcome these limitations this study applies the free and open source hardware approach coupled to distributed digital manufacturing to fabricate a ball mill with a simple, customizable design that can be used in a wide range of scientific applications and circumstances including those without access to reliable grid electricity. The highly-customizable design reduces the cost to <US$130 for an AC powered version and <US$315 for a switchable power that enables off-grid operation with a solar module and battery. Using a solar photovoltaic energy source not only improves the power reliability, but also makes it easier to move the ball mill for use in field environments. The open source ball mill is capable of reducing silicon particle sizes from the millimeter scale down to the nanometer scale.

# Activated charcoal
Source: WikiCiv (rebuilding technology) | https://wikiciv.org/wiki/Activated_charcoal

Activated charcoal, also called activated carbon, is a form of carbon commonly used to filter contaminants from water and air, among many other uses. It is processed (activated) to have small, low-volume pores that increase the surface area available for adsorption.

## Uses

Activated carbon is used in methane and hydrogen storage; air, gold and water purification; capacitive deionization, supercapacitive swing adsorption, solvent recovery, decaffeination, metal extraction, medicine, sewage treatment, air filters in respirators, filters in compressed air, production of hydrogen chloride, edible electronics, and many other applications.

### Industrial

One major industrial application involves use of activated carbon in metal finishing for purification of electroplating solutions. For example, it is the main purification technique for removing organic impurities from bright nickel plating solutions. A variety of organic chemicals are added to plating solutions for improving their deposit qualities and for enhancing properties like brightness, smoothness, ductility, among others. Organic additives generate unwanted breakdown products in solution. Their excessive build up can adversely affect plating quality and physical properties of deposited metal. Activated carbon treatment removes such impurities and restores plating performance to the desired level.

### Medical

Activated charcoal for medical use

Activated carbon is used to treat poisonings and overdoses following oral ingestion. Tablets or capsules of activated carbon are used in many countries as an over-the-counter drug to treat diarrhea, indigestion, and flatulence. However, activated charcoal shows no effect on intestinal gas and diarrhea, and is, ordinarily, medically ineffective if poisoning resulted from ingestion of corrosive agents, boric acid, petroleum products, and is particularly ineffective against poisonings of strong acids or bases, cyanide, iron, lithium, arsenic, methanol, ethanol or ethylene glycol. Activated carbon will not prevent these chemicals from being absorbed into the human body. Incorrect application (e.g. into the lungs) results in pulmonary aspiration, which can sometimes be fatal if immediate medical treatment is not initiated.

### Environmental

Carbon adsorption has numerous applications in removing pollutants from air or water streams both in the field and in industrial processes such as:

- Spill cleanup

- Groundwater remediation

- Drinking water filtration

- Air purification

- Volatile organic compounds capture from painting, dry cleaning, gasoline dispensing operations, and other processes

- Volatile organic compounds recovery (solvent recovery systems, SRU) from flexible packaging, converting, coating, and other processes.

Activated carbon is also used for the measurement of radon concentration in air.

### Agricultural

Activated carbon is an allowed substance used by organic farmers in both livestock production and wine making. In livestock production it is used as a pesticide, animal feed additive, processing aid, nonagricultural ingredient and disinfectant. In organic winemaking, activated carbon is allowed for use as a processing agent to adsorb brown color pigments from white grape concentrates.It is sometimes used as biochar.

### Fuel storage

Research is being done testing various activated carbons' ability to store natural gas and hydrogen gas. The porous material acts like a sponge for different types of gases. The gas may then be desorbed when subjected to higher temperatures and either combusted to do work or in the case of hydrogen gas extracted for use in a hydrogen fuel cell. Gas storage in activated carbons is an appealing gas storage method because the gas can be stored in a low pressure, low mass, low volume environment that would be much more feasible than bulky on-board pressure tanks in vehicles.

### Gas purification

Filters with activated carbon are usually used in compressed air and gas purification to remove oil vapors, odor, and other hydrocarbons from the air. The most common designs use a 1-stage or 2 stage filtration principle in which activated carbon is embedded inside the filter media. Activated carbon filters are used to retain radioactive gases within the air vacuumed from a nuclear boiling water reactor turbine condenser. The large charcoal beds adsorb these gases and retain them while they rapidly decay to non-radioactive solid species. The solids are trapped in the charcoal particles, while the filtered air passes through.

### Chemical purification

Activated carbon is commonly used on the laboratory scale to purify solutions of organic molecules containing unwanted colored organic impurities. Filtration over activated carbon is used in large scale fine chemical and pharmaceutical processes for the same purpose. The carbon is either mixed with the solution then filtered off or immobilized in a filter.

### Mercury scrubbing

Activated carbon, often infused with sulfur or iodine, is widely used to trap mercury emissions from coal-fired power stations, medical incinerators, and from natural gas at the wellhead. However, despite its effectiveness, activated carbon is expensive to use. Since it is often not recycled, the mercury-laden activated carbon presents a disposal dilemma.

## Crafting instructions

Activated charcoal can be activated in one of two common methods of production.

#### Materials needed

Either

- Charcoal

- Sieve

- Furnace

- A source of steam or pure oxygen

or

- Charcoal

- Acid or Strong base

Physical activation:

1. Crush the Charcoal

2. Screen the pieces such that they are small enough that a gas can penetrate the entire piece, but not so small that their packing density prevents gasses from reaching each piece (as may be the case with dust). When finished, all pieces should be roughly the same size.

3. Heat the charcoal between 600-1200°C in the presence of steam or pure oxygen. This can be done by sending the gas through to one side of a container of and exhausting the gas through the other side. Practically, an intake pipe can be fed into a mostly-sealed furnace, in through an open bottom of a container of charcoal and up to the top of the charcoal. Exhaust gasses are free to escape through the bottom of the charcoal and through an exhaust port of the furnace.

4. As this process is endothermic, it is recommended the process is stopped, allowed to heat back up and restarted a number of times.

5. remove the charcoal from the furnace and let cool.

Chemical activation

1. Crush the Charcoal

2. Mix with an acid or strong base. Typical solutions include lemon juice 25% bleach 25% calcium chloride 25%, potassium hydroxide 5%, sodium hydroxide 5%, potassium carbonate 5%, and zinc chloride 25%. The charcoal should be wet, but not saturated.

3. Let sit for 24 hours.

4. Boil for 3 hours. This is the activation step.

5. Once cooled, rinse the charcoal to remove any leftover reagents and let dry.

## Dependencies

- Carbon

- Activated charcoal

- Water

# Ammonia
Source: WikiCiv (rebuilding technology) | https://wikiciv.org/wiki/Ammonia

2D structure of ammonia.

The Haber-Bosch process

Ammonia, also known as hydrogen nitride, is an inorganic compound of nitrogen and hydrogen with the formula NH3. It is a colourless gas with a distinct pungent smell. Although common in nature and in wide use, ammonia is both caustic and hazardous in its concentrated form.

## Materials needed

- Hydrogen

- Nitrogen

- High-purity pulverized magnetite (Fe3O4, a compound of iron)

## Crafting instructions

Note: Due to the high pressure under which this reaction takes place and the reactivity of its reagents, this process is notably dangerous, and should only be attempted under laboratory conditions by professionals

Ammonia production is mainly done through Haber-Bosch process in which atmospheric nitrogen (N2) is reacted with hydrogen (H2) alongside a catalyst of finely divided iron.

The chemical reaction utilized by the Haber-Bosch process is:

N2 +3H2 → 2NH3

ΔH298k = -92.28 kJ per mole of N2

1. The pulverized magnetite (Fe3O4) is oxidized to give magnetite or wüstite (FeO, ferrous oxide) particles of a specific size. The magnetite (or wüstite) particles are then partially reduced, removing some of the oxygen. The resulting catalyst particles consist of a core of magnetite, encased in a shell of wüstite, which in turn is surrounded by an outer shell of metallic iron.

2. Mix one (1) part nitrogen to three (3) parts hydrogen alongside your catalyst.

3. Bring the gas to a pressure of 250 to 350 bar and a temperature of 450 to 550°C

4. Cool the resulting gas to extrude liquid ammonia

## Uses

Around 70% of ammonia is used to make fertilizers in various forms and composition. Ammonia in pure form is also applied directly into the soil. Ammonia, either directly or indirectly, is also a building block for the synthesis of many pharmaceutical products and is used in many commercial cleaning products.

# Anesthesia
Source: WikiCiv (rebuilding technology) | https://wikiciv.org/wiki/Anesthesia

The chemical structure of diethyl ether, the first general anesthetic agent.

Anesthesia is the medical induction of insensibility. This is accomplished through the use of agents called anesthetics. There are two major forms of anesthesia used medically, general and local. General anesthetics work systematically, inducing complete insensibility which appears like a deep sleep. Local anesthetics work locally, inducing a local inability to feel pain, but allowing the patient to remain fully conscious and unsedated. The first safe general anesthetic was diethyl ether and the first local anesthetic was cocaine.

While Ethanol is often thought of as an anesthetic, and while it can technically induce anesthesia by its GABAergic action, it has the major problem that the difference between an effective and lethal dose is hard to determine and its dosage cannot be rapidly changed. Whereas, diethyl ether, being volatile enough to be inhaled, allows rapid reduction or augmentation of the received dose.

## General Anesthetics

A WWI portable anesthetic kit, showing the cotton mask upon which chloroform would have been dropped.

The most easily produced general anesthetic agents to produce are diethyl ether and chloroform. However, of the two, diethyl ether is by far the safer. Chloroform is likely cancerous and has been known to cause sudden cardiac arrest. This is why its use was supplanted by that of ether in hospital settings by the late 19th century. However, chloroform does have one major advantage over diethyl ether which meant it was still used by military surgeons and other surgeons who had to preform surgery in makeshift environments, it is almost entirely nonflammable. Diethyl ether is incredibly flammable, to the extent where there are stories of open bottles of it being lit from candles set on other sides of desks. This, combined with the fact that only hospitals could afford expensive ventilation equipment, meant that it was regarded as a worthwhile tradeoff to allow for a slight chance of cardiac arrest in exchange for the flammability risk to be eliminated.

## Local Anesthetics

The first local anesthetic to be discovered and used clinically was cocaine. In fact, it is still in use today in delicate surgeries involving the nose, as it has the highly desirable quality of acting as both a local anesthetic and a vasoconstrictor (a drug which causes the blood vessels to narrow). This is desirable as it provides a much longer duration of action for the local anesthesia than would otherwise be possible.

However, because cocaine requires the leaves of the tropical coca plant, it is likely to be impractical to use cocaine as a local anesthetic. The second local anesthetic to be discovered, benzocaine, is a convenient substitute as it can be relatively easily produced from aniline, an aromatic feedstock chemical which is one of the backbones of pharmaceutical chemistry.

# Anvil
Source: WikiCiv (rebuilding technology) | https://wikiciv.org/wiki/Anvil

(selectively transcribed from Wikipedia, still needs work.)

An anvil is a metalworking tool consisting of a large block of metal (usually forged or cast steel), with a flattened top surface, upon which another object is struck (or "worked").

Anvils are massive because the higher their inertia, the more efficiently they cause the energy of striking tools to be transferred to the work piece. In most cases the anvil is used as a forging tool.

The great majority of anvils are made of cast steel that has been heat treated. Inexpensive anvils have been made of cast iron and low-quality steel, but are considered unsuitable for serious use, as they deform and lack rebound when struck.

## Structure

The primary work surface of the anvil is known as the face. It is generally made of hardened steel and should be flat and smooth with rounded edges for most work. Any marks on the face will be transferred to the work. Also, sharp edges tend to cut into the metal being worked and may cause cracks to form in the workpiece.

The horn of the anvil is a conical projection used to form various round shapes and is generally unhardened steel or iron. The horn is used mostly in bending operations.

The step is the area of the anvil between the "horn" and the "face". It is soft and is used for cutting; its purpose is to prevent damaging the steel face of the anvil by conducting such operations there and so as not to damage the cutting edge of the chisel, though many smiths shun this practice as it will damage the anvil over time.

The hardy hole is a square hole into which specialized forming and cutting tools, called hardy tools, are placed. It is also used in punching and bending operations. These are not to be confused with swage blocks, although their purpose is similar.

The pritchel hole is a small round hole that is present on most modern anvils. Some anvils have more than one. It is used mostly for punching. At times, smiths will fit a second tool to this hole to allow the smith more flexibility when using more than one anvil tool.

#### Suitable Materials

The face is hardened and tempered to resist the blows of the smith's hammer, so the anvil face does not deform under repeated use. A hard anvil face also reduces the amount of force lost in each hammer blow. Hammers, tools, and work pieces of hardened steel should never directly strike the anvil face with full force, as they may damage it; this can result in chipping or deforming of the anvil face.

A common blacksmith's anvil is made of either forged or cast steel, forged wrought iron with a hard steel face or cast iron with a hard steel face. Cast iron anvils are not used for forging as they are incapable of standing up to the impact and will crack and dent. Also, cast iron anvils without a hard steel face do not have the rebound that a harder anvil would and will tire out the smith.

Cheap anvils made from inferior steel or cast iron are considered unsuitable for serious use. Amateur smiths have used lengths of railroad rail, forklift tines, or even simple blocks of steel as makeshift anvils.

#### Placement

The anvil is placed as near to the forge as is convenient, generally no more than one step from the forge to prevent heat loss in the work piece. An anvil needs to be placed upon a sturdy base made from an impact and fire resistant material. Common methods of attaching an anvil are spikes, chains, steel or iron straps, clips, bolts where there are holes provided, and cables.

# Aspirin
Source: WikiCiv (rebuilding technology) | https://wikiciv.org/wiki/Aspirin

## Overview

Aspirin is a salicylate, a similar molecule, salicin, was originally extracted from the bark of willow trees, and has a broad range of clinical uses - from pain relief, to survival benefits in heart attacks. It can be consumed orally or applied topically. Overdose can cause significant metabolic disturbances, and is life threatening.

## Clinical Uses

### Contraindications

#### Pregnancy

During pregnancy, the foetal circulation depends on the presence of prostaglandins to maintain added pathways between the right and left sides of the heart. Aspirin will block these prostaglandins and can cause the foetus to die.

#### Bleeding disorders or significant active bleeding

Aspirin has some anti-platelet activity, which can slow clotting. Generally the effect is mild, but careful consideration should be given to those patients for whom bleeding is likely, or where active bleeding is posing a risk to their life.

#### Gastric ulcers or vomiting blood (haematemesis)

Used frequently, aspirin can predispose patients to the formation of gastric ulcers, and should ideally be taken alongside food to help limit this side effect.

Kidney failure

While difficult to measure directly in a post-modern medicine situation, patients with signs of kidney failure should receive reduced doses and frequency, ideally avoiding the drug where possible. There is a risk of the drug metabolites building up in the body, which can cause toxicity.

#### Children (<16 years)

There is a risk of Reye's syndrome, which causes brain and liver swelling, and has a high mortality rate. Especially avoid aspirin in children recovering from a viral illness.

### Dosing

#### Pain relief (oral)

240-300 mg (milligrams) THREE times daily, aim to limit use to maximum of 3 days to minimise side effects on stomach + kidneys

#### Migraine

900 mg ONCE only as a single dose.

(exquisite diagnostic caution should be taken to avoid other malignant headaches, such as subarachnoid haemorrhage or intracerebral haemorrhage, as aspirin would worsen bleeding)

# Axe
Source: WikiCiv (rebuilding technology) | https://wikiciv.org/wiki/Axe

A metal axe

An axe is a tool used to shape, split or cut wood, and as a weapon. The axe has many forms and specialised uses but generally consists of an axe head with a handle, or helve.

## Materials needed

- Large rock with perpendicular edges. Alternatively, a river rock, round shaped and smooth to the touch.

- Green wood, meaning that it that has been recently harvested and therefore has not had an opportunity to dry, or from a young or small tree. This will ensure the wood can be bent and shaped without breaking.

- Cord, made of bark or wet rawhide.

## Crafting instructions

### Shaping and sharpening the axe head

To craft the axe head, hit off a piece of the source rock for the head with a hammering rock that can be held comfortably in your hand and with an even rock face, striking the upper edge of the former with the latter. While hitting it, you should focus on striking off flakes and slabs of rock, using swift hits. You might want to wear safety eye and hand protection for safety to protect yourself from flying rock shards. The stone head should be around the size of your palm or a little larger, and it can take several hours until the source rock has the ideal size for the head.

If a river rock is used, its grains have to be slowly removed to prevent it from damaging or splitting. Hit a small area of the rock at a time, using short and swift strokes to peck away the river rock carefully. The work might take several hours.

Once the rock has been pecked down to an appropriate size, you will need to form the cutting edge of the axe head, sharpening it. The head should taper down toward the edge, and the edge should be a similar thickness to a metal axe, with a narrow edge. Hold the axe head vertically, resting on the wide side of the rock, and use the hammering rock to form a narrow end of the axe head by hitting it with small and short movements. Afterwards, polish and sharpen the axe head on a stone with water, since it helps that it stays sharp and durable.

### Attaching the head to the handle

To insert the axe head, split the axe handle, trying to make a symmetrical split and that is not leaning to one side. This can be done by using a knife or a sharp stone. A method to attach the axe head to the handle is splitting the handle down the middle, forming a gap large enough to fit the axe head. Gently slide the axe head into the split once it is large enough to fit the head. If you are using a knife, you can carve out a symmetrical split in the handle, ensuring that the width of one both sides of the handle are the same. Alternatively, if the wood is very flexible and soft, the top of the handle can be soaked in water until it is it is soft and pliable, making sure that it does not break, and afterwards it can be bent it around the axe head, attaching it to the handle.

Whether method is used, the axe head needs to be secured to handle with cord to prevent it from falling out when the axe is used. The cord can be made of bark or wet rawhide. To ensure that the cord is strong and durable, it should be twisted by looping one piece of cord over the other to form the twisted cord; it should be long enough to wrap around the axe head several times. Wrap the cord tightly around the axe head, crossing the cord over so it forms an "X" shape on the axe head, repeating the process twice and then tying the cord around the handle several times.

If there is access to fire, the head can also be secured in the handle without cord by burning a hole through the handle. Following this method, you need to use a sharp rock and a piece of wood to hammer in a hole a few centimeters or inches from the top of the handle, or make one with a knife; the hole should be wide enough to fit the head. This can be measured by holding the head against the side of the handle and marking its width on the handle. A long piece of wood then needs to be heated up with fire until it is burning and smoking. Blow on it until you have a hot point, and run the point around the handle's hole to smooth it out and shape it, so it fits the axe head well. Once the hole is shaped, place the head into the hole with the sharpened edge facing upward. Use a piece of wood to knock the axe head into the handle. Hold the axe head with a hand and make a few swift knocks on the head with the piece of wood until it is in place. You should check that there are small gaps of space between the sides of the head and the handle hole. Small gaps prevent the handle from splitting when the axe is used.

## Care

The following are some recommendations to use and take care of the axe:

- Chip at a right angle, a few degrees off of 90º degrees of the wood. A steeper angle prevents side-slap (when the axe head hits against the handle and object), which can break the stone head. Stone axes are unlike metal axes and cannot be struck at the same angle. Swing the axe slightly off of a direct vertical drop if the wooden piece is sitting horizontally. An anvil (a wooden block) can also be used underneath the object, which help keeps the object from bouncing and increases the efficiency of the axe.

- Use a leather sheath to transport the axe, which prvents nicks and can protect the axe from objects falling against it.

- Use oil in the handle to prevent it from drying too fast and from damaging. Extra virgin oil is recommended. The handle can also be oiled twice before storing.

- Keep the axe head secure, which must remain tight against the handle. A loose head can cause side-slap, which in turn might break the handle from the dual impact.

- Replace the axe head exactly as it was if you need to remove it.

- Peck and ground down any nicks or gouges in the axe to preserve the cutting angle. Stone axes usually get nicked or gouged when used on a rocky surface.

## Safety

A safe distance between bystanders of around 3 meters (10 feet) should be kept, since stone axes cause debris to fly further than metal counterparts, up to almost 12 meters (40 feet), with varying speed velocities depending on the axe size and swing force that can cause serious injuries.

# Emergency Medicine
Source: WikiCiv (rebuilding technology) | https://wikiciv.org/wiki/Emergency_Medicine

Emergency medicine is a medical specialty focused on diagnosing and treating patients with sudden, severe, and potentially life-threatening injuries or illnesses. It involves rapid assessment, evaluation, and intervention, with the goal of preventing further disability or death. Emergency medicine encompasses a wide range of settings, from hospital emergency departments to pre-hospital care and major events.

## Bleding control

Severe bleeding, also known as hemorrhage, is a life-threatening condition that requires immediate action. In emergency situations, controlling the bleeding quickly can be the difference between life and death. Three essential techniques for managing severe bleeding are wound packing, pressure dressings, and the use of a tourniquet.

Wound Packing

Wound packing is used when bleeding occurs in areas where a tourniquet cannot be applied, such as the groin, armpit, or neck. To perform wound packing, a sterile gauze or hemostatic dressing is firmly inserted deep into the wound to apply direct pressure on the bleeding vessel. This helps to promote clotting and stop the bleeding. After the wound is fully packed, continuous pressure should be applied until medical help arrives.

Pressure Dressing

A pressure dressing is used to maintain constant pressure over a wound to help control bleeding. After packing a wound or if the bleeding is less severe, a sterile dressing is placed on the wound and then tightly wrapped with an elastic bandage or similar material to hold the pressure. This method helps to stabilize the patient while waiting for professional medical treatment.

Tourniquet Use

A tourniquet is used for life-threatening bleeding on limbs when direct pressure and wound packing are not sufficient. The tourniquet is placed 5–7 cm (2–3 inches) above the bleeding site—never on a joint—and tightened until the bleeding stops. It must be clearly marked with the time of application, as prolonged use can cause tissue damage. Tourniquets are highly effective in preventing death from uncontrolled extremity bleeding when used correctly

It is important to keep the patient warm to prevent hypothermia.

## Heimlich maneuver

Important Note:

The Heimlich maneuver is only suitable for conscious or unconscious individuals over 1 year old who cannot speak, cough, or breathe.

For infants under 1 year, pregnant women, or severely overweight individuals, modified techniques are required.

Heimlich Maneuver Step by Step (for Adults & Children approx. 1 Year and Older

### 1. Assess the Situation

Determine whether the person is choking and unable to speak, cough, or breathe.

Ask: “Are you choking?”

No response and no air = Emergency.

### 2. Give 5 Firm Back Blows

Stand slightly to the side and behind the person.

Support their upper body as they lean forward.

Deliver 5 firm back blows between the shoulder blades using the heel of your hand.

### 3. Apply the Heimlich Maneuver (if needed)

If the back blows don’t work:

How to perform it:

- Stand behind the person.

- Wrap your arms around their upper abdomen.

- Make a fist with one hand.

- Place the fist between the navel and the breastbone.

- Grasp the fist with your other hand.

- Perform quick, upward abdominal thrusts (“J-thrust” motion).

- Repeat up to 5 times.

### 4. Alternate Between Back Blows and Heimlich Maneuver

Repeat alternating 5 back blows and 5 abdominal thrusts until the object is expelled or the person becomes unconscious.

### 5. If the Person Becomes Unconscious

Gently lay them on the ground.

Begin CPR (cardiopulmonary resuscitation) immediately:

- 30 chest compressions → 2 rescue breaths.

- If you cannot or do not want to give breaths: hands-only CPR (100–120 compressions per minute).

Ensure emergency help is on the way!

Important Notes:

- Do not use the Heimlich maneuver on infants under 1 year old!

- Do not practice it casually – it can cause injury.

- After a successful maneuver, medical examination is essential, as internal injuries may occur.

# Knots
Source: WikiCiv (rebuilding technology) | https://wikiciv.org/wiki/Knots

Assortment of knots

Knots have thousands of potential uses, both practical and decorative. Knots can be tied using rope, string, or any other type of cordage. Practical knots include hitches, bends, loops, splices, and lashings. Hitches secure a rope to a pole or other stationary object. Bends are used to join two ropes. Loops create a loop. Splices create a joint between ropes or parts of the same rope, typically by untwisting and interweaving strands. Lashings are used to connect two or more poles together and are particularly useful for structure construction.

## Terminology

### Running End

The running end is the end of the rope that is used to tie a knot. This end is also called the working end.

### Standing Part

The standing part refers to all of a rope that is not the running end.

### Overhand Loop

An overhand loop is a loop in which the running end of the rope is on top of the standing part.

### Underhand Loop

An underhand loop is a loop in which the running end of the rope is under the standing part.

### Bight

A bight is a U-shaped section of rope formed by doubling-back the rope against itself. The running end does not cross the standing part.

### Turn

A turn is formed by wrapping a rope around a pole once. The running end will be in the opposite direction of the standing part after the turn is formed

### Roundturn

A roundturn is formed by wrapping a rope around a pole one and a half times. The running end will exit the roundturn towards the standing part.

### Lashing Terminology

#### Pole

A pole is the structural component of constructions made with lashings. These are typically wooden and usually small trees or straight branches, but any material can be used.

#### Spar

A large, heavy pole

#### Wrap

A turn made around two or more poles to hold the poles tightly together. Most lashings require three or more wraps made tightly side by side.

#### Frap

A frap goes around the wraps to pull them together.

## Knots

### Hitches

Hitches are used to secure a rope to objects, including but not limited to poles, tent stakes, trees, in some cases people, or through a hole in an object like a grommet on a tarp. The term connection point is used in the following instructions instead of pole, hole, object, etc.

#### Half-Hitch

Completed half-hitch knot.

The half-hitch is very easy to tie, but is not particularly strong or reliable. However, it is an important step in the tying of many other knots.

1. Pass the running end of the rope around the connection point to form a bight.

2. Bring the running end under the standing part forming an underhand loop.

3. Bring the running end up and over the standing part and through the loop.

4. Tighten the half-hitch and slide it against the connection point.

#### Two Half-Hitches

Illustration of tying two half hitches. The first image depicts step 1. The second image depicts step 3. The third image depicts the finished knot.

The two half-hitches knot is a simple, relatively reliable hitch and is a great general purpose option if easy adjustability or excess strength are not required.

1. Pass the running end of the rope around the connection point to form a bight.

2. Bring the running end under the standing part forming an underhand loop.

3. Bring the running end up and over the standing part and through the loop. This makes a half-hitch.

4. Make another underhand loop (away from the connection point from the first half-hitch) and again wrap the running end up and through the new loop forming the second half hitch. Be sure both wraps around the standing part are in the same direction.

5. Tighten the knot and slide it against the connection point.

#### Taught-Line Hitch

The taught-line hitch is also useful for tying a rope to an object, but creates an adjustable loop that can be used to tighten the line. This is particularly useful for tents or flys.

1. Pass the running end of the rope around the connection point and under the standing part to form an underhand loop.

2. Bring the running end over and around the standing part and through the loop.

3. Go around the standing part again inside the loop towards the connection point.

4. Bring the running end away from the connection point and form a new underhand loop outside of the original loop.

5. Bring the running end up, around, and through the new underhand loop to tie another half-hitch. Be sure to wrap in the same direction for all of the loops.

6. Tighten the knot around the rope and work any slack out of it.

7. Slide the taut-line hitch to tighten or loosen the line.

#### Bowline

Bowline knot tying.

The bowline knot forms a reliable loop that will not slip. The bowline knot is technically a loop knot, but it can be used as a strong reliable hitch when strength is needed. The bowline knot tied around the waist can be used in rescue situations in a pinch.

1. Form a bight around (or large enough to fit around) the connection point.

2. Make an overhand loop in the standing part of the rope.

3. Pass the running end up through the loop.

4. Pass the running end behind and then around the standing part.

5. Bring the running end back through the loop. The running end should exit the knot toward the inside of the loop, and parallel to where it entered the loop.

6. Tighten by pulling the standing part away while holding the bight and running end.

#### Clove Hitch

Clove hitch knot

The clove hitch is frequently used along with lashings.

1. Make a turn around the connection point.

2. Make the first half of a second turn around the connection point, crossing over the first turn to form the shape of an X. (The running end will be next to the standing part, but on the opposite side of the connection point after this half turn)

3. Finish the second turn, but tuck the running end under the X. The ends of the rope should come out between the legs of the X.

4. Pull the ends of the rope to tighten the hitch.

#### Timber Hitch

The timber hitch is typically used to drag logs and is also used to start diagonal lashings. The timber hitch will stay secure as long as there is tension on the rope, but it will loosen and can be removed easily as soon as the tension is removed.

1. Pass the running end of the rope around the connection point.

2. Loop the running end around the standing part.

3. Twist the running end around the loop around the connection point at least 3 times (use more loops depending on the strength needed).

4. Pull the slack out of the rope and tighten the knot against the connection point.

### Bends

Bend knots are used to tie ropes together. These can be different parts of the same rope or to connect multiple ropes.

Sheet bend knot

#### Sheet Bend

The sheet bend is a useful general knot for tying two different ropes together. It is similar to the bowline but consists of two separate ropes. The sheet bend can be used to connect ropes of the same or different diameter. In the following instructions, rope 1 should be the thicker rope in the case of different diameters.

1. Make a bight in the end of rope 1.

2. Pass the running end of rope 2 through the bight.

3. Pass the running end of rope 2 behind and around both sections of rope 1.

4. Bring the running end of rope 2 across the front of the bight in rope 1 and tuck it under its own standing part.

5. Tighten b holding the bight and pulling the standing part of rope 2.

Square knot.

#### Square Knot

The square knot is an easy, general purpose bend. It works best when tied around something and the knot is pressed against the surface of the object. The ropes used in a square knot should be of the same diameter, and it should not be used to hold a heavy load.

1. Hold a rope in each hand.

2. Pass the right end over and under the rope in your left hand. The right rope is now on the left and the left is now on the right.

3. Pass the rope the is now in your left hand over and under the rope that is now in your right hand.

4. Tighten by pulling both running ends at the same time.

This can be remembered by "right over left, left over right." The ends of each rope will be on the same side of the knot.

There are several similar knots related to the square knot. The Granny Knot is tied by going right over left, right over left which results in a twist rather than the square shape. The Granny knot pulls apart much more easily than the square knot, but it works in a pinch for something unimportant. The Thief Knot looks almost identical to a square knot, except that the running ends will end on opposite sides of the knot. Again, this is less secure than a square knot, but it can be used to see if someone has untied your knot and inadvertently tied it back with a square knot.

#### Surgeon's Knot

The Surgeon's knot is an improved square knot. The Surgeon's knot is used to tie sutures, and is particularly useful when tying small plastic lines like fishing line. To tie a surgeon's knot, tie a square knot, but in step 2 pass the right end over and under the left end twice instead of once.

### Lashings

Example of square lashing. The fraps are clearly visible between the poles and around the wraps. Ideally, every lash in this image should be pulled as tight as possible.

#### Square Lashing

Square lashing is used to bind together two poles that contact each other and cross at an angle between 45-90 degrees. The wrapping turns are at 90 degrees to the poles, thus the name square lashing.

1. Place one pole on top of another pole at the desired angle.

2. Tie a clove hitch around the bottom pole below the intersection.

3. Make a wrap around both poles going from the clove hitch, over the top pole, under the bottom pole, back over the top pole on the opposite side, and under the bottom pole below the clove hitch.

4. Repeat step 3 at least 3 times laying the rope outside of the previous wrap each time.

5. Make at least 2 fraps around the wraps and pull extremely tight. These fraps go around the ropes, essentially in between the poles and are used to pull the wraps as tight as possible.

6. Finish with a clove hitch around the top pole as tight as possible against the lashing.

#### Shear Lashing

Shear lashing is useful for constructing the initial section of a triangle. It is constructed with two poles side by side which are lashed and then spread apart to form an angle. The bottom of the triangle can be attached with square lashing.

1. Lay two poles side by side and tie a clove hitch to one of them.

2. Wrap in a figure 8 pattern around both poles.

3. Repeat several times. The stiffness of the angle is determined by how many wraps are made and how tight those wraps are.

4. Make 2 tight fraps between the poles around the wraps to pull the wraps together.

5. Finish with a clove hitch on the other pole as tight as possible against the lashing. Test that you can make the desired angle before tying and loosen or tighten the fraps and wraps as needed.

6. Spread the ends of the poles.

#### Diagonal Lashing

Diagonal lashing is used to bring poles that don't touch together, typically when adding supports onto an existing frame - for example, connecting the middle of an X of cross-beams onto a square frame. It is called diagonal lashing because the lashings are diagonal to the poles and make an X or + pattern depending on the direction of the poles. These instructions will assume you are connecting poles arranged as an X.

1. Tie a timber hitch around both poles and pull it very snug.

2. Make three tight wraps around both poles in the vertical direction.

3. Make three tight wraps around both poles in the horizontal direction.

4. Make two tight fraps between the poles to pull the wraps tight.

5. Finish with a clove hitch on one of the poles as tight as possible against the lashing.

#### Tripod Lashing

Tripod lashing is similar to shear lashing, but uses three poles to form a tripod rather than two poles to form part of a triangle. Tripod lashing is useful for hanging a pot over a fire.

1. Lay three poles next to each other and ensure the bottom ends are lined up.

2. Tie a clove hitch to one of the poles on the outside.

3. Wrap the rope around the poles going over one, under the next, and around the outside poles. The wraps should be weaved through the poles.

4. Repeat step 3 several times. The stiffness is determined by how many wraps are made and how tight those wraps are.

5. Make two fraps around the wraps on both sides of the middle pole.

6. Finish with a clove hitch on the other outside pole as tight as possible against the lashing. Test the positioning and spread of the tripod and tighten or loosen the lashing as needed before tying.

#### Round Lashing

Round lashing is used to join two poles together to extend their length.

1. Position the poles end to end with enough overlap to make a secure lashing. The overlap depends on the size of the poles and strength required, but if you want to build a giant tower it should probably be at least a foot or two.

2. Tie both poles together with a clove hitch close to the end of one of the poles.

3. Make many, very tight wraps around the poles, laying each wrap next to the last as close as possible. These wraps need to be as tight as you can possibly make them.

4. Finish with another tight clove hitch around both poles.

5. Make a second round lashing close to the end of the other pole to prevent bending. You should have two round lashings on the overlap between the poles.

#### Floor Lashing

Floor lashings are used to make surfaces like tabletops, raft decks, walkways, and obviously floors. Floor lashings are made from two spars and as many floor poles as needed to fill the platform.

1. Lay the floor spars parallel to each other and the desired distance apart.

2. Lay the floor poles perpendicular on top of the spars and line them up side by side.

3. Tie a clove hitch around one of the spars.

4. Wrap the running end from the outside of the spar up and over the first floor pole then down and back to the inside of the spar.

5. Wrap the running end around the spar and back up on the outside around the second floor pole. Repeat this wrapping up from the outside and down to the inside for each floor pole.

6. Finish with a clove hitch as close to the final floor pole as possible.

7. Repeat the lashing for the opposite side.

# Mirror
Source: WikiCiv (rebuilding technology) | https://wikiciv.org/wiki/Mirror

Bronze mirror, New Kingdom of Egypt, Eighteenth Dynasty, 1540–1296 BC, Cleveland Museum of Art (U.S.)

Mirrors are objects with reflective surfaces.

## History

The earliest manufactured mirrors were produced by polishing stone such as obsidian, a volcanic glass. Obsidian's high melting point would prevent prehistoric people from reshaping these mirrors so they would likely be small. Obsidian is also brittle so these mirrors would be fragile.

In the Bronze Age, mirrors started being produced by polishing pieces of copper, tin, silver, or other metals. Metals are more durable than stone and can more easily be reshaped, but tarnish with exposure to air and would need to be polished again over time.

In the Middle Ages, mirrors started being produced by depositing tin onto sheets of glass. The glass would be used as the front surface of the mirror to protect the metal and prevent tarnishing. Mercury vapor was often used to deposit the tin, making this process hazardous to workers.

In the Industrial Revolution, mirrors started being produced by reducing a solution of silver nitrate in water onto sheets of glass (used in Tollen's test). This remains the most widely used method of mirror production as the amount of silver required is very low, keeping costs reasonable.

Since 1920, mirrors for optical and scientific purposes have been made by sputtering metals such as aluminum directly onto glass. This is more expensive than the wet silver deposition process so is not found in standard mirrors.

Parabolic mirrors shown reflecting and focusing sunlight.

## Fire Starting

Concave mirrors such as parabolic mirrors can be used to focus sunlight and ignite carbon-containing materials.

# Tourniquet Use
Source: WikiCiv (rebuilding technology) | https://wikiciv.org/wiki/Tourniquet_Use

### Required Materials (Improvised):

- Rod or sturdy object (e.g., screwdriver, wooden stick, pen without casing – must be strong and unbreakable)

- Strong strap or fabric strip (e.g., scarf, belt, long piece of fabric, tubular bandage, strip of T-shirt)

- Optional: Cord, string, or tape to secure the rod

### Instructions: How to Apply a DIY Tourniquet

#### 1. Choose the correct location:

- Use only for life-threatening bleeding on arms or legs.

- Apply the tourniquet at least 5–7 cm (2–3 inches) above the wound – never directly over a joint!

#### 2. Wrap the band around the limb:

- Wrap the band (fabric strip or belt) tightly around the limb.

- Pull it as tight as possible and tie a simple knot.

#### 3. Insert the rod (windlass):

- Place the rod on top of the knot.

- Tie another knot over the rod to secure it in place.

#### 4. Twist the rod until the bleeding stops:

- Twist the rod to increase pressure on the limb.

- Keep twisting until the bleeding significantly slows or stops.

- This will be painful – but it is necessary to save a life.

#### 5. Secure the rod:

- Once the bleeding has stopped, secure the rod in place using string, another strap, or by tucking it into the band.

#### 6. Note the time:

- Write down the time the tourniquet was applied (e.g., on the patient’s skin or clothing).

- A tourniquet should not stay on for more than 1–2 hours – only longer in absolute emergencies.

### Important Notes:

- Tourniquets are a last resort for severe arterial bleeding when direct pressure and pressure dressings are not enough.

- Improper use can lead to nerve damage or tissue loss – use only in real emergencies.
